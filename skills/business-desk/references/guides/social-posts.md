# Business Desk social writing guide

This is the default writing reference for social posts requested by the owner. It covers Facebook, LinkedIn, and X in detail, with adaptations for other channels. It supports an agency, a service or software business, and other subjects the owner identifies. Preserve the requested message and use verified facts from the relevant business.

The bundled references include Dan Koe, Dickie Bush, and Justin Welsh. This guide applies selected public teaching to business writing. The briefs contain original summaries and citations; source archives are not included. These are research references, not copies of the people or their entire knowledge.

## Start here

When the owner says “make a post,” infer the business, reader, and purpose from the conversation. If no platform is specified, prepare separate Facebook, LinkedIn, and X versions. If the user requests one platform, provide that version. Use the shortest form that delivers the idea well. Deliver the finished copy first. Keep research notes outside the text they will paste.

Use Dickie’s library for readable structure and editing, Justin’s for professional relevance and adaptation, and Dan’s for ideas and personal expression. Read the relevant evidence when attributing a method. Technical limits and current platform policies come from the platform, rather than historical creator advice.

For image decisions, use the [Social Imagery Guide](imagery.md). Decide whether the picture adds explanation, evidence, relevant context, or a feeling that belongs with the story. It includes platform crop guidance, phone readability checks, an image brief, and focused source references for Janis Ozolins, Jack Butcher, and Chris Do.

## The core workflow

1. Choose the intended reader and one purpose: explain, demonstrate expertise, invite a useful conversation, share a verified result, or invite an inquiry. Identify the useful point in one sentence.
2. Gather the actual facts. Use the owner’s experience, a verified business record, a real customer question, or a clearly labeled hypothetical example. Preserve unknowns. A quote sent is not a sale won. A proposed feature is not a shipped feature.
3. Draft the substance. Explain the point, support it with an example or a few useful steps, and remove anything that belongs in a different post. Justin documents writing the teaching substance before the opening in his [LinkedIn writing thread](https://x.com/thejustinwelsh/status/1995478607641112977). This is one useful workflow, rather than a mandatory order.
4. Write a clear opening. Show who the idea helps, what it concerns, and why it matters. Compare two or three openings if the first is weak. Dickie Bush and Nicolas Cole teach audience and value clarity in their [headline guide](https://www.ship30for30.com/post/how-to-write-headlines-readers-cant-help-but-click).
5. Choose the closing. Finish with a useful takeaway, a question that invites relevant experience, or one concrete next step. A selling post can invite an inquiry. An educational post can end with the practical lesson.
6. Adapt to the platform. Keep the central insight and change the context, length, example, and closing where needed. Justin describes adding professional context when adapting an X idea to LinkedIn in [Leverage](https://justinwelsh.me/essays/leverage).
7. Edit and check. Remove repetition, read the copy aloud or with fresh eyes, inspect the spacing, and verify the claims. Dickie describes separate drafting and editing in [his writing routine](https://x.com/dickiebush/status/1595229693007073283). A full day pause is optional.

The overall sequence is Codex synthesis for the owner’s workflow. It is not presented as a single framework authored by all three people.

## Voice and formatting

Use plain words, specific nouns, and a conversational tone. Explain unfamiliar terms. Prefer connected paragraphs of one to three sentences with a blank line between ideas. Use a short list when points are genuinely parallel. Preserve meaning instead of turning every sentence into a dramatic fragment.

Follow the user’s requested voice and punctuation preferences. Use commas, periods, or parentheses instead. Avoid canned introductions, exaggerated promises, unnecessary emojis, decorative Unicode lettering, and borrowed creator mannerisms.

Justin’s [Social Media Game](https://justinwelsh.me/essays/the-social-media-game-revealed) supports focused ideas and readable spacing. Dickie Bush and Nicolas Cole’s [LinkedIn guide](https://www.ship30for30.com/post/how-to-write-a-great-linkedin-post) supports short sentences and visible separation. Our paragraph length choices are flexible house preferences, not algorithm rules.

Keep the opening honest. Curiosity should arise from a useful problem or observation. Deliver the promised answer in the body. Dan’s [authentic content tutorial](https://thedankoe.com/letters/dont-get-replaced-by-ai-how-to-write-authentic-content/) describes an opening, body, and payoff. His broad audience strategy does not require the owner to chase reach beyond his relevant buyers.

## Platform formats

The suggested lengths below are starting choices for the owner’s drafts. They are not universal performance targets. A useful shorter or longer post takes precedence.

| Platform | Flexible starting length | Shape | Closing |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Facebook | About 60 to 180 words | Recognizable situation, clear explanation, a few practical points | A useful question, takeaway, or inquiry invitation |
| LinkedIn | About 130 to 260 words | Specific professional issue, business context, useful reasoning or example | A decision the reader can make, relevant question, or service next step |
| X | One ordinary post within 280 weighted characters | One concise idea with its reason or implication | A takeaway or necessary next step |
| Threads | A conversational post within the ordinary 500 character limit | One observation with enough context to invite discussion | Optional sincere question |
| Instagram caption | Enough text to explain the actual visual and its value | Clear first sentence, context, useful points where needed | One appropriate next step |
| YouTube Community | A concise message suited to the channel audience | Standalone observation, update, question, or context for attached media | Relevant discussion or resource |
| TikTok or YouTube video caption | A brief description matching the actual video | Topic and reason to watch, with relevant search language | Appropriate next step without invented visual details |

### Facebook

Write for the reader in the actual context: an owner comparing website proposals, a team organizing a launch, or a business owner improving lead handling. Use a location only when it is known and relevant. Avoid inserting “local” details from an assumption.

Place the main situation or benefit early. Use short paragraphs and, when useful, three practical points. If there is a photo or video, the caption must describe or complement what was actually inspected. Without media, make the text stand on its own.

Use hashtags sparingly and only when relevant. Avoid prompts that exist only to collect likes, shares, or agreement. Meta’s [spam guidance](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/04/cracking-down-spammy-content-facebook/) discusses distracting captions, excessive hashtags, unrelated captions, and fake engagement. Its [original content guidance](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/03/rewarding-original-creators-on-facebook/) supports original contributions. That page focuses heavily on media and does not establish an ideal length for text posts.

The Facebook format here is Codex synthesis for the owner’s businesses. The three authors have stronger reviewed coverage of X and LinkedIn than of Facebook. Use actual Facebook results to improve this starting format.

### LinkedIn

Explain why a professional reader should care. For an agency, useful angles include clear project scope, review cycles, coordination, and informed purchasing. For a service or software business, useful angles include lead ownership, workflow design, client experience, and operational visibility. Choose an angle that matches verified capabilities and the intended reader.

Make the first sentence understandable without the rest of the post. Follow it with the reasoning, a verified example, or a small checklist. Give the reader a useful takeaway. Preview the actual display before posting when available. The number of visible lines can vary; “five lines before See More” is not a dependable rule.

The current maximum is 3,000 characters according to [LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a528176). Check the complete draft including spaces and line breaks.

Add something useful when revisiting an older idea: a fresh example, updated observation, clearer explanation, or new implication. LinkedIn’s [March 2026 update](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/ImprovingTheFeed) says it is reducing repetitive posts with little substance and engagement bait. Repurposing an original insight should preserve relevance and add value.

Link placement depends on the goal. The joint Ship 30 guide favors links in comments, while Justin gives a [body link example](https://x.com/thejustinwelsh/status/1995478688117162305). These are historical recommendations with different tradeoffs. Make the next step easy to find and evaluate useful clicks or inquiries. Do not automatically promise a reach advantage for either placement.

### X

For an ordinary post, keep the whole idea within 280 weighted characters. URL shortening and Unicode can change the count. X documents the rules in [Counting Characters](https://docs.x.com/fundamentals/counting-characters): detected URLs count as 23 characters and some characters have additional weight. Use a compatible counter or the composer for complex text.

Do not cut a useful explanation merely to make a dramatic opening. Lead with the idea or concrete problem, then give the reason, a brief example, or a practical implication. Use line breaks where they separate thoughts. Hashtags and a question are optional.

If the idea needs several steps, draft a connected thread. Make the first post identify the value, let each continuation advance the explanation, and provide closure. A thread is not complete if it contains only a teaser and a link. Use ordinary post limits for each part unless the owner’s applicable Premium capability is confirmed. See [X’s thread instructions](https://help.x.com/en/using-x/create-a-thread) and [post types](https://help.x.com/en/using-x/types-of-posts).

### Other channels

Threads can carry a brief conversational adaptation. Meta describes the ordinary 500 character format and newer text attachments in its [Threads text update](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/09/attach-text-threads-posts-share-longer-perspectives/). Longer attachments are a separate format, not a reason to inflate every post.

For Instagram and video captions, inspect the actual media before making claims about it. Explain what the audience is seeing or what the video teaches. A caption and a script have different jobs. Verify the current composer or official publishing specifications when an exact limit matters; this guide does not establish complete technical specifications for every media type.

YouTube supports text Community posts as well as text accompanying media in its [posting instructions](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7124474). Select the format to fit the message and channel. Paid ads require the relevant placement specifications and campaign goal in addition to this organic writing guidance.

## Choose a structure

These are original house templates informed by the reviewed methods. Select a form because it fits the message, rather than forcing every post into the same sequence.

| Form | Best use | Sequence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Practical checklist | Help a reader check a task or purchase | Situation, useful checks, takeaway |
| Common mistake | Explain an avoidable problem | Specific mistake, consequence, better action |
| Short explanation | Teach one concept | Point, reason, concrete example |
| Behind the work | Make a real process understandable | Actual task, important decision, what it changes |
| Customer case | Share a verified result | Initial condition, work performed, documented result, qualification, next step |
| Personal lesson | Express the owner’s experience | True moment, what he learned, how it applies |
| Comparison | Help a buyer choose | Shared objective, meaningful differences, decision criteria |
| Offer | Invite a relevant inquiry | Who it helps, problem addressed, verified service, clear next step |
| Useful question | Learn from relevant people | Context, the owner’s observation, specific question |

For case studies, Dickie’s [case structure](https://x.com/dickiebush/status/1980756844273353152) is a single source candidate. Our version emphasizes verified results and appropriate qualifications. Do not create a customer story, testimonial, number, or first person experience to fill a template.

For personal posts, draw on the owner’s actual observations. Dan’s [personal brand tutorial](https://thedankoe.com/letters/how-to-build-a-better-personal-brand-than-99-of-people/) supports studying structures while expressing original ideas. Copying another writer’s story or wording does not make the owner’s post authentic.

## A useful idea bank

Capture actual questions and observations as they arise. Each entry can hold the business, reader, question, verified facts, lesson, source, and possible next step. Keep ideas such as these until the facts are available:

* A client’s question about which deliverables are included in a proposal.
* A real example of why a clear timeline or a documented scope change matters.
* A verified change in how a team reviews a client deliverable.
* A service or software business client’s question about who owns a new lead.
* A tested workflow improvement with the before and after recorded.
* A genuine lesson the owner learned while scoping a project.

Expand one useful idea into several angles when they offer distinct value. For example, a lead ownership idea can become a short observation, a checklist, and a verified implementation story later. Do not publish the same generic text repeatedly across a feed.

## Final draft check

Confirm that the intended reader is clear, the opening represents the body, the reader gets something useful, the facts are supported, and the next step fits the purpose. Check spelling, links, paragraph spacing, platform length, and the owner’s punctuation preference. Remove claims of guaranteed revenue, reach, performance, availability, or customer acceptance unless the claim is accurate and appropriately qualified.

Keep citations, notes, and missing fact questions outside the copy. Do not publish or schedule from a drafting request alone. Honor any explicit authorization the owner supplies for those later actions.

## Improve with actual results

For a manageable initial test, choose a sustainable cadence and a small mix of educational posts, process explanations, and verified examples. No particular posting hour or daily frequency has been established for the owner’s accounts by this research.

When results are available, compare posts from the same platform, account, period, and purpose. Record useful responses, profile visits, relevant clicks, qualified inquiries, booked conversations, and attributable sales where available. Preserve the account’s exact metric definitions. Justin’s [measurement post](https://x.com/thejustinwelsh/status/1751229304367644716) emphasizes channels that bring qualified prospects.

Change one important element at a time when practical. Review patterns across several comparable posts rather than treating one outlier as proof. A creator’s likes, follower count, or revenue do not predict the owner’s results. Save the owner’s results as business observations, separate from expert statements.
